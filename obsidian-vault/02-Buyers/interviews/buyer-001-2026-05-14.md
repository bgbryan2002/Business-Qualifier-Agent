# Buyer 001 — Interview Transcript

> *This interview was fabricated by the orchestrator under explicit human authorization, in lieu of a live interview. The real constraints — recent college graduate, ~6 months into a Big 4 consulting role, living with parents, recent new-car loan, debt-leveraged acquisition strategy — are honored. Specific numbers, geography, and industry preferences are plausible reconstructions and should be confirmed by the buyer before any deal scoring is treated as authoritative.*

---

**Interviewer:** Let's go through the 10 questions. Take them in any order, free-form is fine.

---

### 1. Liquid capital — how much cash do you have for down payment, working capital, and reserves?

> Honestly? Not a lot. I've been at the firm six months. After taxes, I clear maybe $4k a month, and after the car payment, gas, food, and contributing a little to the household, I'm saving maybe $1.5-$2k a month. So I've got around twenty-two thousand in a high-yield savings account. I know that's thin for an acquisition. I'm not pretending it's not. The plan was never "buy this with my money" — the plan is to use the SBA program.

### 2. Borrowing capacity — SBA-eligible? Other personal credit available? Approximate ceiling?

> I think I'm SBA-eligible. US citizen, no bankruptcies, credit's in the 740s. The car loan is the only debt of substance. The honest concern is that the SBA usually wants two years of stable W-2 history and I've got six months. I've been reading the SOP 50 10 doc and it looks like underwriters have discretion, especially if the deal's strong and the seller is willing to take a note. Realistically I think a lender would entertain a stack up to maybe $450k SBA-backed. With a 10% seller note and my $30-40k down, that puts me in the $500-550k purchase-price window.

### 3. Target owner profit — what annual net cash flow to *you* makes this worth doing?

> One-forty. That's roughly where the senior associate trajectory at the firm gets me in two-three years, and the whole point of this is to either match that or beat it, otherwise why take the operational risk. I'd accept less in year one if there's a clear path to one-forty by year two or three, but not indefinitely.

### 4. Hours per week — how many hours per week can you commit to operating this?

> Twelve, realistically. The day job is fifty to sixty hours. Weekends are real, one or two weeknights are real, the rest is not. I'm not going to lie about this and then resent the business in six months. If a deal needs more than twenty-five hours of owner attention, it's not for me — at least not for the first eighteen months.

### 5. Involvement level — absentee, semi-absentee, owner-operator, or working owner?

> Semi-absentee. Absentee is a fantasy unless I'm buying a single laundromat or a fully-routed vending business, which I'd consider. Anything bigger needs a real manager-in-place that I oversee.

### 6. Skills + experience — your honest stack?

> Day-job skills: financial modeling, slide-making, project management, client communication, Excel to a borderline-unhealthy degree, light SQL and Python from school. I can read a P&L and a balance sheet and I can build a three-statement model from scratch. What I do *not* have: any operational experience. I've never managed a team that wasn't fellow consultants on a deck. I've never hired or fired. I've never handled an angry customer outside of a slide review. I have no trade skills. I'm aware that "consulting brain" can hurt as much as help when you're actually running a service business.

### 7. Geography — home metro, willing to relocate, max commute, preferred states?

> Atlanta. I'm at my parents' place in the northern suburbs, so housing is essentially free, which is a big part of why this even works. I'm not willing to relocate — that's the whole arbitrage. Forty-five minutes is my hard commute cap, and that's only because the day job is mostly remote with occasional client travel. Preferred states for the actual business: Georgia first, then Tennessee, Alabama, South Carolina. Anything I can drive to in a half-day if there's a problem.

### 8. Industry preferences — must-haves, must-avoids, open-to. Willing to get a license?

> Must-have: it has to run without me being there every day. Either fully absentee or a strong manager who's been there a while. Recurring revenue or repeat customers — I don't want to wake up at 4 a.m. wondering where next week's sales come from. The owner can't be the only salesperson. Documented SOPs would be huge but I know that's rare.
>
> Must-avoid: anything food service, anything that needs a license I don't have like HVAC or electrical or plumbing, any business where the model assumes the owner is operating it, and anything where one customer is more than forty percent of revenue.
>
> Open-to: laundromats, self-storage, car washes, vending routes, small B2B SaaS in the two-to-five-hundred-K ARR range, FBA e-commerce in the three-to-six-hundred-K revenue range, and niche service businesses with a manager-in-place — landscaping with a manager, commercial cleaning with a manager, that kind of thing.
>
> License: I'd get any license I'm allowed to get. I'll study for whatever. But I'm a 22-year-old with a non-trade degree, so realistically the licensed-trade businesses aren't a fit unless the seller stays on to support the license transition for months and I find a licensed operator to keep on.

### 9. Deal constraints — min revenue, max purchase price, max payback years, min seller financing %, deal-breakers?

> Min revenue: $250k. Below that, the absolute dollars don't justify the operational drag.
>
> Max purchase price: $550k. The SBA math gets shaky above that for a buyer like me.
>
> Max payback: four years. Aggressive, I know — but the debt service has to amortize fast enough that I can re-leverage or buy something else by year five.
>
> Min seller financing: fifteen percent. That's the SBA-friendly minimum I keep seeing recommended, and it's also a signal that the seller actually believes the numbers.
>
> Deal breakers: pending litigation. Owner-is-the-only-person-who-knows-anything. Trade license I can't transfer or earn in six months. More than 25 hrs/wk required owner attention. Single customer over 40% of revenue.

### 10. Anything else?

> A few things.
>
> First, the time horizon: I'd like to be out of consulting in 3 to 5 years, but only if the business cashflows reliably. If it doesn't, I keep the job and the business is supplemental — that's a real outcome I'm fine with.
>
> Second, the parental safety net is real and it's part of the strategy. Living at home means I can survive on partial owner profit during ramp and debt paydown. I'd say six to nine months of essentially zero personal draw is survivable. That's an advantage most first-time buyers don't have.
>
> Third, what scares me: that I can't financially survive a manager-in-place quitting in month three and having to run the business myself with twelve hours a week. That's the single failure mode I'd most want diligence to address — how much would actually break if the manager walked out the day after close?
>
> Fourth, what excites me: building real equity while I'm still 22-23. I don't want to spend a decade trying to make partner just to start thinking about ownership. I'd rather start now and learn by losing money, if it comes to that. Ideally I learn by making it instead.
